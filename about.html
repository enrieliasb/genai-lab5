The problem

A prospective packaging customer rarely arrives with one meaning of “sustainable.” They may be asking about recycled fiber, about whether a paper-based pack can replace another material, about greenhouse-gas emissions, or about what happens to the package after use. Those are different questions. A first meeting goes better when the customer and the specialist are looking at the same published facts.

Smurfit Westrock publishes a sustainability approach. It is still a lot of ground to cover before someone is ready to talk about a real pack. The gap this prototype explores is that briefing: a short, careful conversation that stays inside what the company has already said, so the specialist call can start further along.

The guide is not a salesperson, a specification tool, or a spokesperson. It does not design a box, price one, or speak for Smurfit Westrock. It prepares a customer to read the published approach, and it says so out loud.

How the guide works

The guide is a conversation with a fixed shape. A visitor should always know which step they are in, and the answer should always be traceable to a public page when the point depends on one. The path is welcome, then a question, then a grounded answer with a source link, then a check — “Was this helpful? Yes/No” — then room to ask another.

That sequence is the product. The welcome sets the boundary. The question is in the visitor’s words. The answer stays inside published material and carries a link when a claim needs a source. The yes/no check is a pause, not a survey for the company. If the answer missed, the visitor can ask again or leave for a specialist. If it helped, they can ask another question in the same thread.

  1. Welcome

    The guide introduces itself as a sustainability briefing and names the kinds of questions it can take. It does not open with a product pitch.

  2. Question

    The visitor asks in their own words. The home page offers three sample questions that stay inside the published record.

  3. Grounded answer with a source link

    The reply uses only what Smurfit Westrock has published, and it includes a link to an official page when the point depends on that page. Two starting points are the sustainability overview and the reporting page.

  4. Was this helpful? Yes/No

    The guide asks whether the answer landed. Yes keeps the thread open. No is a signal to rephrase, to read the source, or to stop and talk with a person.

  5. Ask another

    The visitor can continue, or they can leave the guide when the next question needs a quote, a design, or anything the published pages do not decide.

The three pillars

Smurfit Westrock describes its sustainability strategy under three pillars: Planet; People and Communities; Impactful Business. The guide is allowed to name them and to point at the public description. It is not allowed to invent metrics for any of them.

Planet

The company describes a circular model that uses renewable, recyclable, and recycled materials for paper-based packaging. That is a description of the model, not a guarantee that every package is recyclable in every place.

People and Communities

People and communities are a named pillar beside planet and business impact. This guide does not add workforce figures, community claims, or stories the published briefing on this site does not carry.

Impactful Business

The company describes this pillar as the business side of the strategy. The guide does not translate it into a price, a certification, or a carbon saving for a customer’s own product.

Its Better Planet Packaging initiative focuses on packaging innovation, circularity, and collaboration. The guide can describe that focus. It cannot treat the initiative’s name as proof that a particular pack meets a standard.

Primary source: smurfitwestrock.com/sustainability.

The 2030 target, with the limits attached

In May 2026, with its 2025 Sustainability Report, Smurfit Westrock announced a 2030 target to reduce Scope 1 and 2 greenhouse-gas emissions 28% versus a 2019 baseline. The sentence is easy to over-read. The guide is written to keep the limits in the same breath as the number.

Target, not a result

28%

Scope 1 and 2, versus 2019

A 2030 target announced with the 2025 Sustainability Report. It is not a report of emissions already reduced, and it is not a Scope 3 goal.

  • It is a target, not an achieved result.
  • It covers Scope 1 and Scope 2 greenhouse-gas emissions.
  • The comparison point is a 2019 baseline.
  • It is not a Scope 3 goal.
  • It is not a product-level carbon saving for any package a customer might buy.

Source: smurfitwestrock.com/sustainability/reporting.

Guardrails on claims

The useful version of this guide is the one that refuses. If a sentence would only be true with a number, a certificate, or a local rule the published pages do not give, the guide does not guess.

  • Not claimedProduct-level carbon savings.
  • Not claimedCertifications for a package, a mill, or an order.
  • Not claimedPrices.
  • Not claimedLead times.
  • Not claimedOrder status.

Recyclability gets the same treatment. The company describes paper-based packaging in a circular model that uses renewable, recyclable, and recycled materials. A visitor who asks “Is every paper-based package recyclable where I live?” should hear no. Collection rules differ by package and by place. The guide points back to the published approach instead of inventing a local answer.

Customer handoff

Quote and design questions leave the guide. They belong on Smurfit Westrock’s official inquiry forms. The company’s contact page includes a general Get in Touch form, and it directs a product quote toward the inquiry form on the relevant product page so the request reaches the right team.

The handoff is part of the concept, not a failure of the chat. A good answer on the pillars or the 2030 target can end with a clear next step: read the source, or talk with a specialist if the question is now about a real pack. The guide does not pretend to be that specialist.
